Why Miniature Highlands?

August 13, 2026

When we started looking at cattle for the place in Round Top, the usual options were on the table — commercial breeds, some of the popular heritage breeds, even a few of the smaller dual-purpose types. None of them felt quite right.

We wanted cattle. We also wanted animals we could actually manage as we get older. Full-size Highlands are beautiful, but they’re still big animals. Commercial cattle require a different level of infrastructure and daily work than we were prepared to take on for a small, personal herd.

Miniature Highlands solved the problem. They keep the classic look — the long hair, the quiet expression, the sturdy Highland character — in a package that is significantly easier to handle. The temperament reputation is well earned. These are calm, curious, people-oriented cattle when they’re raised with consistent, quiet handling.

A few practical advantages stood out for us:

Size. Lower feed requirements, easier fencing, simpler handling facilities, and less strain on the land. On a modest acreage under a wildlife management plan, that matters.

Disposition. We are selecting hard for gentle, confident animals. The goal is cattle that are a pleasure to be around, not just productive.

Hardiness. Highlands were developed in a tough climate. They handle weather well, do fine on good forage, and generally stay healthy with basic care.

Presence. Even at a smaller size, they still look like Highlands. That visual character is part of what we wanted on the property.

We’re not trying to reinvent the breed or chase extreme sizes. We want sound, correct, calm miniature Highlands that can thrive in Texas and make good foundation animals for other small herds. Polled genetics are a preference when we can get them without sacrificing other priorities.

That’s the short version of why these cattle made sense for Round Top Fold. As the first animals arrive and we start learning the day-to-day reality, we’ll share more of what we discover.

— Drew
